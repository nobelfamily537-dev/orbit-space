Artemis III: How NASA Plans to Put Humans Back on the Moon

Updated 26 October 202612 min read
Share WhatsApp Telegram X Facebook

On 23 October 2026, NASA held a briefing that a lot of people had been waiting years for. For the first time, the agency laid out in detail how it actually intends to put astronauts back on the Moon — not a concept, not an animation, but a mission architecture with dates, spacecraft, crews and a plan for how the pieces fit together.

The short version: NASA is targeting a landing on the lunar surface in **2028**, with a demonstration flight called Artemis III in **summer 2027**. In between sits one of the most complicated engineering problems the agency has attempted in decades — docking two different commercial landers with the Orion spacecraft, in orbit, before either of them has flown with people on board.

This is what the plan actually says, and what still has to go right for it to work.

What Artemis III is now

Artemis III has changed shape several times, and the version NASA described in October is not the one people were expecting two years ago.

It is now an **Earth-orbit test flight**. Four astronauts will launch on the Space Launch System rocket and fly a planned 14-day mission that never leaves Earth orbit. Their job is to rendezvous and dock with test versions of the two commercial lunar landers NASA has contracted — SpaceX's Starship Human Landing System and Blue Origin's Blue Moon — and prove that the hardware talks to each other.

The crew is Randy Bresnik, Frank Rubio and Andre Douglas from NASA, joined by Luca Parmitano of the European Space Agency. It is a small crew for a very large amount of work.

NASA says it is still aiming for summer 2027, though officials have been careful not to commit to a specific date. Administrator Jared Isaacman has described the schedule as achievable, and in September he acknowledged the mission was roughly 90 days behind where it had been. Artemis III in its current form only came into existence in February 2026, so a slip of a few months is less dramatic than it sounds.

Artemis IV: the actual landing

The landing itself is now **Artemis IV**, targeted for 2028. This is the mission where astronauts leave Earth orbit, travel to the Moon, transfer into a lander and set foot on the surface.

NASA has said it will decide **which company lands astronauts first** — SpaceX or Blue Origin — based on lander readiness in 2028. That is a notable change. It turns the two contracts into a genuine race rather than a fixed sequence, and it gives both companies a reason to move.

The new architecture, and why it changed

The most consequential change in the October briefing was where the spacecraft meet.

The old plan had Orion docking with the Starship lander in a **near-rectilinear halo orbit** — a wide, looping path around the Moon. It was elegant on paper and difficult in practice, because it demands more energy and more precise timing.

The new plan is simpler. SpaceX will fuel a Starship lunar lander in low Earth orbit, then dock with Orion in **high Earth orbit**. The crew then flies to the Moon inside Orion, and two of the four astronauts transfer into Starship for the descent to the surface.

Blue Origin's route is different again. It involves **four New Glenn launches**: three transfer stages and the Blue Moon Mark 2 lander, assembled in low Earth orbit and then sent to an elliptical polar orbit around the Moon, where Orion meets it. Notably, the Blue Origin plan requires **no spacecraft-to-spacecraft refuelling** — a significant advantage in complexity, even if it costs more launches.

Both companies are now freed from the halo orbit requirement, and the reason is straightforward: **NASA cancelled the Lunar Gateway** earlier in 2026. Gateway was a small station in that same halo orbit, and with it gone, there was no longer a reason to send crews to that part of space.

The refuelling question

If there is one number that could still derail the 2028 date, it is how many tanker launches Starship needs to reach the Moon.

On 22 October, a member of NASA's Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel said the working estimate was around **22 tanker flights** to fill a propellant depot in orbit. Elon Musk responded publicly that the figure was far too high, and that the real number would be closer to **6 to 8**.

NASA declined to give a precise number during the briefing. Isaacman described Musk's figure as possible, but as the "ultimate potential end state" of Starship rather than where the vehicle is today.

The honest position is that nobody knows yet. It depends on how much payload Starship can actually lift, how much cryogenic methane and liquid oxygen boils off while sitting in orbit, and how efficiently propellant can be transferred from one ship to another. None of those have been demonstrated at the scale a lunar mission requires.

That is why the number matters. Every tanker flight is another launch that has to work, another chance for weather or hardware to intervene, and another line in the schedule.

Risks the safety panel flagged

NASA's Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel met a day before the briefing and raised concerns that are worth taking seriously.

The panel questioned the objectives and architecture of Artemis IV and beyond, including three specific issues: lander readiness, the plan to require only **one uncrewed landing test per provider** before astronauts fly, and the fact that Artemis will involve the **first use of cryogenic propellants on the lunar surface**.

That last point is easy to underestimate. Keeping liquid hydrogen and oxygen cold on the Moon — through a lunar day and night, in vacuum, with no infrastructure — is a problem nobody has solved in flight. The panel said it would formally recommend that NASA re-examine Artemis IV's objectives and system architecture.

NASA's response at the briefing was confidence. Isaacman said the agency has "the mandate, the resources, and the support from our stakeholders."

Where they are going

The landing site target is the **lunar south pole**. It is not a sentimental choice.

The south pole holds permanently shadowed craters where water ice is thought to survive, and nearby ridges that receive sunlight for most of the lunar year. Ice means water, water means drinking water, breathable oxygen and — split into hydrogen and oxygen — rocket propellant. Sunlight means power. It is the only part of the Moon where both are plausibly available close together.

It is also brutally difficult terrain. The Sun sits near the horizon, shadows are long and deep, and temperatures swing by hundreds of degrees.

There is a smaller problem too, and NASA is currently paying for a solution. Walking on the lunar surface builds up static charge, and in shadowed regions a spacesuit can accumulate a strong negative charge. NASA has described the risk as an astronaut becoming "a walking, high voltage capacitor" — touching a lander could trigger an arc that damages the suit or shocks the crew. The agency has opened a **$150,000 Lunar Grounding Challenge** for designs that safely discharge an astronaut, with submissions due in January.

Why this matters beyond the United States

The Artemis programme is often described as an American return to the Moon, but its architecture is unusually international. Artemis III includes an ESA astronaut. Blue Origin's lander work involves European suppliers. And the scientific questions — polar ice, lunar geology, whether the Moon can support a permanent presence — are shared.

For India, the comparison is unavoidable and interesting. ISRO's Chandrayaan-3 landed near the south pole in 2023 at a fraction of Artemis's cost, using a single self-contained mission rather than an orbital assembly of commercial hardware. Gaganyaan is building India's independent human spaceflight capability, and the Bharatiya Antariksh Station is planned for the 2030s.

The two programmes are not competing so much as solving different problems. NASA is trying to build a reusable, sustained transport system. ISRO is building sovereign capability at low cost. Both are aiming at roughly the same patch of the Moon.

Timeline

  • **2026** — SLS rolls to Launch Pad 39B for a propellant-loading test
  • **Summer 2027** — Artemis III: four astronauts, 14 days, Earth orbit, docking with both landers
  • **2028** — Artemis IV: the first crewed landing, provider chosen on readiness
  • **2030s** — a sustained lunar base

The honest assessment

The plan NASA described in October is more concrete than anything the programme has published before, and that alone is progress. The architecture is simpler than the version it replaced, the roles are clearer, and the decision to pick a first lander on readiness has introduced real competitive pressure.

But the schedule still depends on things that have not happened yet: orbital propellant transfer at scale, a crewed docking with two unproven landers, and cryogenic storage on the lunar surface. Any one of those slipping pushes 2028 out.

The most useful way to read the announcement is not as a promise but as a statement of intent with dates attached. NASA has now told everyone exactly what it is trying to do. The next two years will show whether the hardware agrees.

Frequently asked

**When will humans land on the Moon again?** NASA is targeting 2028 with Artemis IV.

**What is Artemis III?** An Earth-orbit test flight in summer 2027 where four astronauts dock with test versions of the SpaceX and Blue Origin landers.

**Who is on the Artemis III crew?** Randy Bresnik, Frank Rubio and Andre Douglas of NASA, and Luca Parmitano of ESA.

**Why was the Lunar Gateway cancelled?** NASA cancelled it in 2026, which removed the requirement to meet in a near-rectilinear halo orbit and simplified both lander architectures.

**Is Starship the lander?** Starship HLS is one of two contracted landers. Blue Origin's Blue Moon is the other, and NASA will choose which flies crew first based on readiness.
