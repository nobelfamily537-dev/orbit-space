Titan: Saturn's Moon With Rivers, Lakes and Rain

Updated 12 November 20268 min read
Share WhatsApp Telegram X Facebook

Titan is the second largest moon in the solar system and the only one with a real atmosphere. It has clouds, rain, rivers, lakes and seas. It also has a surface temperature of about −179°C, so none of that liquid is water.

On Titan it rains methane. The rivers run with ethane. The seas near the north pole are wider than the Caspian. And somewhere under the ice, there is very likely an ocean of liquid water.

It is the strangest familiar place we know of.

A world with weather

Titan's atmosphere is about 95 percent nitrogen with most of the rest methane. The surface pressure is roughly 1.5 times Earth's, which means a person standing on Titan would feel more air than they do at sea level at home, in a gravity about one seventh as strong.

Because the atmosphere is so thick and the gravity so low, a human with strapped-on wings could genuinely fly there. It is one of the few places in the solar system where that is true.

The methane in the atmosphere is what makes the weather. Sunlight breaks methane molecules apart, and the pieces recombine into heavier hydrocarbons that form a thick orange haze. That haze is why Titan looks like a featureless orange ball through a telescope.

Lakes, rivers and seas

The Cassini spacecraft spent thirteen years at Saturn and mapped Titan with radar that could see through the haze. What it found was a landscape shaped by liquid: river channels, deltas, flooded valleys, and large standing bodies of liquid concentrated near the north pole.

Kraken Mare is the largest, roughly the size of the Caspian Sea. Ligeia Mare and Punga Mare are smaller but still substantial. The liquid is a mixture of methane and ethane with dissolved nitrogen, and it behaves much like water does on Earth, just far colder.

There is a full cycle: liquid evaporates, forms clouds, rains out, carves channels and pools in basins. The difference is that the working fluid is a fuel, not water.

Huygens

On 14 January 2005 the European Space Agency's Huygens probe, carried to Saturn by Cassini, descended through Titan's atmosphere and landed on the surface. It was the first landing in the outer solar system, and the most distant landing ever achieved.

The probe transmitted for about 72 minutes on the surface before the relay link with Cassini was lost. Its images showed a flat plain strewn with rounded ice pebbles, and a horizon of low hills. Channels cut across the ground, dark and dry.

Huygens proved that the chemistry and the landscape were real, not an interpretation of radar data. Almost everything we know about Titan's surface began with that hour.

The chemistry question

Titan's haze is made of complex organic molecules called tholins, formed when sunlight and charged particles break apart methane and nitrogen and let the fragments recombine. These are the same kinds of molecules that, on the early Earth, are thought to have been the raw material for life.

Nothing on the surface could be alive in the way we understand it: the temperature is far too low for the chemistry of life as we know it, and there is no liquid water. But the surface is a giant natural laboratory running prebiotic chemistry for billions of years, and we can go and look at it.

Underneath, the picture changes. Measurements of Titan's gravity and its interaction with Saturn's magnetic field suggest a layer of liquid water beneath an ice shell, possibly tens of kilometres down. If that ocean exists, and if it touches rock, it has the same three ingredients Europa and Enceladus have: water, chemistry and energy.

Dragonfly

NASA's Dragonfly mission is a rotorcraft lander, a flying drone the size of a small car, designed to fly between sites on Titan's surface rather than drive. Rotor flight makes sense there: the thick atmosphere and low gravity make it much easier than on Mars.

Dragonfly is planned to launch in 2028 and arrive in 2034, landing near the equatorial dunes. From there it will make a series of flights of up to a few tens of kilometres, sampling the surface chemistry at each stop and eventually reaching the Selk impact crater, where liquid water and organic material may once have mixed.

It carries a mass spectrometer, a gamma-ray and neutron spectrometer, cameras and a meteorology package. It is not looking for life directly. It is looking at the chemistry, which is the question you have to answer first.

Why Titan matters

Every other place we are searching for life is a search for water. Titan is a search for what happens before life: a world with all the organic chemistry, all the energy gradients, and all the complexity, running for four billion years without ever getting wet enough to finish the job.

If we find the chemistry halfway to biology there, it tells us something about how likely life is everywhere else. If we find nothing, that tells us something too.

Either way, it is the only moon with weather, the only moon with seas, and the only place in the solar system where a human could strap on wings and fly. That is worth a drone.
