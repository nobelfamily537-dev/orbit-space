Are We Alone? The Search for Life in Space

Updated 20 October 20269 min read
Share WhatsApp Telegram X Facebook

Look up on a clear night and you are looking at a few thousand stars. Our galaxy alone holds around two hundred billion of them, and we now know that most stars have planets. Somewhere in all that, the odds say, life should exist somewhere other than Earth. And yet we have never found a single confirmed example of it. That gap — between what should be out there and what we have actually seen — is the most interesting open question in science.

What do we mean by "life"?

When scientists search for life, they are not looking for spaceships or signals from little green figures. They are looking for something much smaller and much more likely: microbes.

Single-celled life appeared on Earth very early — within a few hundred million years of the planet forming — and then spent roughly three billion years as the only life here. If life exists elsewhere, the odds strongly favour it being simple, not intelligent. So the search is mostly for bacteria, algae, or chemical traces of something alive.

The three ingredients

Every living thing on Earth needs the same short list:

  • **Liquid water** — not ice, not vapour, but liquid
  • **The right chemistry** — carbon, plus elements like hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen
  • **An energy source** — sunlight, or chemical energy from rocks

That is it. No magic ingredient. And all three are common in the universe. Water is everywhere: in the polar craters of the Moon, in the clouds of Jupiter, in the dust between stars. Carbon is the fourth most common element in the galaxy.

Where are we looking?

**Mars** is the closest candidate. It once had rivers, lakes and a thicker atmosphere. Today it is cold and dry, but there is water ice at its poles and under its surface. NASA's Perseverance rover has been collecting rock samples and is looking for signs that Mars was once habitable. A future mission is planned to bring those samples back to Earth.

**Europa**, a moon of Jupiter, is a ball of ice with a deep salty ocean underneath. The ocean may hold twice as much water as all of Earth's oceans combined. NASA's Europa Clipper spacecraft is on its way there now to study the ice and the ocean below.

**Enceladus**, a small moon of Saturn, is even more surprising. The Cassini spacecraft flew through plumes of water shooting out of its south pole and found organic molecules in them. There is a warm ocean under that ice, and we have already tasted it.

**Titan**, Saturn's largest moon, has rivers, lakes and seas — but of liquid methane, not water. It is the only other place in the solar system with liquid on its surface. Whether life could exist in such a cold, strange liquid is an open question.

**Exoplanets** — planets around other stars — are the newest and biggest target. We have confirmed more than five thousand of them, and some sit in the "habitable zone", where liquid water could exist on the surface. In 2019, astronomers found water vapour in the atmosphere of one of them, K2-18b.

The most exciting phrase in science is not "Eureka!" but "that's funny..." — because that is usually where a discovery starts.

How do we actually look?

We cannot visit these places easily, so we look from a distance and with robots.

  • **Rovers and landers** dig into soil and test chemistry directly
  • **Orbiters** photograph surfaces and map ice and minerals
  • **Spectroscopy** splits the light from an exoplanet's atmosphere and reads which gases are present — including gases that, on Earth, are only made by living things
  • **Radio telescopes** listen for signals that could not be produced by nature

That last one is SETI — the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence. It has been listening since 1960, and so far it has heard nothing but silence and noise.

The Fermi paradox

In the 1950s, the physicist Enrico Fermi asked a simple question about all this: **"Where is everybody?"**

If the galaxy is old and full of planets, and if life is common, then someone should have spread across it by now. We should see something — signals, structures, evidence. We see nothing. This contradiction is called the Fermi paradox, and there are many possible answers. Maybe life is rare. Maybe intelligence is rarer still. Maybe civilisations do not last long. Or maybe we simply have not looked properly yet.

What have we found so far?

Honestly: nothing confirmed. No microbe, no fossil, no signal.

But we have found the conditions. Organic molecules in space. Water on moons and planets. Thousands of worlds where life could, in principle, exist. In 2023, the James Webb telescope detected possible signs of a molecule linked to life on an exoplanet — but the result is not confirmed, and it may have a non-biological explanation.

That is where we are: surrounded by possibility, with no proof.

Why it matters

Finding life elsewhere would be the biggest discovery in human history. Even a single confirmed microbe on Europa or in a Martian rock would tell us that life is not a freak accident — that it happens wherever the conditions are right. And that would mean the universe is full of it.

Not finding it would be just as interesting. If Earth is genuinely alone, then this small wet planet is the only place in the known universe where anything is alive, and we should probably treat it better.

When might we know?

Soon, by the standards of science. Europa Clipper arrives in the 2030s. Mars sample return is planned for the same decade. New telescopes will be able to study the atmospheres of small, rocky exoplanets in detail for the first time.

The answer to "are we alone?" may not come from a signal. It may come from a tiny chemical fingerprint in the air of a planet nobody has ever seen, read off a graph by someone in a laboratory on Earth.

For now, the honest answer is: we do not know. But for the first time in history, we have the tools to actually find out.
