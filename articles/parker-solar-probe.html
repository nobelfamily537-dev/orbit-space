Parker Solar Probe: The Fastest Object Humans Have Ever Built

Updated 27 November 202611 min read
Share WhatsApp Telegram X Facebook

On 4 September 2026, a small spacecraft the size of a car passed 6.1 million kilometres from the surface of the Sun. It was moving at 692,000 kilometres per hour. In the roughly 24 hours around that closest approach it covered almost 40 percent of the Sun's circumference.

Nothing built by humans has ever moved faster. Nothing has ever been closer to a star and sent back data.

That was Parker Solar Probe's 29th close approach. The record it matched was set on 24 December 2024, and it has now equalled it more than half a dozen times.

The numbers, plainly

The Earth orbits the Sun at about 107,000 kilometres per hour. Parker reaches roughly 692,000, or about 6.4 times faster. At that speed, a flight from Delhi to London would take a little over twenty seconds.

Its closest approach of 6.1 million kilometres is about 4 percent of the Earth-Sun distance, which is 150 million kilometres. For comparison, Mercury never comes closer than about 46 million kilometres to the Sun's surface.

At that distance, the intensity of sunlight is roughly 475 times what it is at Earth. The front of the spacecraft faces temperatures of about 1,400 degrees Celsius, hot enough to melt steel many times over, while the instruments behind the shield sit near room temperature.

How it survives

The answer is a heat shield 11.4 centimetres thick, made mostly of carbon foam sandwiched between carbon fibre sheets. The outer surface is coated in white alumina ceramic, which reflects as much sunlight as possible. The foam is 97 percent air, which makes it an excellent insulator and very light.

Almost nothing protrudes past the shield. The solar arrays are cooled by a system that circulates deionised water, and they are designed to retract and hide behind the shield during the closest passes, when there is not enough sunlight reaching them to be useful anyway.

This is why the spacecraft can spend a few days inside the Sun's outer atmosphere and still work. The shield is a shadow that the rest of the probe lives in.

What it found: switchbacks

The solar wind is a constant stream of charged particles flowing out from the Sun. At Earth it is a fairly steady breeze. Close to the Sun, Parker found it is anything but.

Within about 24 million kilometres of the Sun, the spacecraft kept flying through sharp, S-shaped folds in the magnetic field, where the field direction flips back on itself and then flips back again. They were named switchbacks, and they arrived in clumps, far more often than anyone expected.

As Parker spiralled closer, it pinned down where they come from: patches on the Sun's visible surface where magnetic funnels form. In 2024, scientists announced that the fast solar wind, one of the two main classes of solar wind, is at least partly powered by these switchbacks. That helped close a puzzle that had been open for about fifty years.

Crossing into the corona

In 2021, at about 13 million kilometres from the surface, Parker crossed a boundary that had only ever been inferred from a distance: the edge of the corona, the Sun's outer atmosphere. It found the boundary uneven and more complex than models had suggested, with the spacecraft dipping in and out of it rather than crossing a clean line.

Then came the images. In July 2025, NASA released the closest photographs ever taken of the Sun, captured by the WISPR instrument during the December 2024 pass. They show the corona and the solar wind in visible light, including the heliospheric current sheet, the surface where the Sun's magnetic field flips from pointing north to pointing south.

They also captured something never seen before at high resolution: several coronal mass ejections colliding and piling up on top of each other. When CMEs merge, their trajectory can change and their magnetic fields can tangle, which makes them harder to predict and potentially more dangerous to satellites, astronauts and power grids.

Two kinds of slow wind

Fast solar wind is the easier problem. Slow solar wind travels at roughly half the speed, and its origin was murkier.

Before Parker, distant observations suggested there were two varieties of slow wind, distinguished by the behaviour of their magnetic fields. One type, called Alfvénic, contains small switchbacks. The other, non-Alfvénic, does not. Parker confirmed both exist, and its close-up views are helping separate where each comes from. The non-Alfvénic wind may originate at helmet streamers, the large loops connecting active regions. The Alfvénic wind may come from near coronal holes, the dark, cooler regions of the corona.

There is also a subtler result. The Sun's surface vibrates in five-minute oscillations, sound waves trapped in its convection zone. In 2025, researchers reported the first direct in-situ detection of those oscillations in the upper corona, seen as large-amplitude wave trains at about 3.2 millihertz, at a significance near six sigma. The Sun's surface ringing reaches out into the solar wind.

Why it matters on Earth

Space weather is not an abstraction. A large coronal mass ejection can induce currents in power grids, disrupt GPS and radio, force airlines to reroute polar flights, and expose astronauts to radiation. In 1989, a geomagnetic storm took out the Hydro-Québec grid for nine hours.

Forecasting those events requires understanding the solar wind at its source, which is exactly what Parker measures. Its data feeds models that try to predict, hours or days ahead, whether an eruption will hit Earth and how hard.

The Sun is currently in an active phase of its eleven-year cycle, which makes the timing useful as well as lucky.

What happens next

Parker is out of new orbit options. It has reached the closest distance its design allows, and will keep repeating the same orbit, matching the same records, while its instruments keep working.

In late 2026, the mission's next steps were formally under NASA review. That is normal for an extended mission, and it is not a signal that anything is wrong with the spacecraft. But it does mean the programme is now in the hands of a budget process rather than an engineering one.

What is not in question is the result. A spacecraft designed to survive a few close passes has now made twenty-nine of them, and each one returned data from a region no other instrument can reach. The record for the fastest human-made object is unlikely to be broken by anything else this decade.
