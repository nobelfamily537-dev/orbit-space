The 2027 Total Solar Eclipse: The Eclipse of the Century

Updated 28 October 202614 min read
Share WhatsApp Telegram X Facebook

On the afternoon of Monday, 2 August 2027, the sky over Luxor in southern Egypt will go dark at one o'clock. Not dim. Not overcast. Dark enough for stars, with a black hole where the Sun should be and a ring of white fire around it.

It will stay that way for six minutes and twenty-two seconds.

That single number is why astronomers have been calling this the eclipse of the century since the path was first calculated. There has not been a total solar eclipse this long over land that ordinary people can actually reach in more than a century, and there will not be another until 2114.

Why it is called the eclipse of the century

Three things make 2 August 2027 unusual.

The duration. Totality reaches 6 minutes 22–23 seconds in Egypt — more than double the roughly two-and-a-half minutes most total eclipses deliver. The last total eclipse of the 21st century that lasted longer was on 22 July 2009, at 6 minutes 39 seconds, but its maximum fell over the open Pacific. The longest anyone could stand on land that time was a remote, uninhabited island in Japan that requires special permission to visit. In 2027, the longest totality happens over a desert that tour buses can reach.

The location. The path crosses southern Spain, Gibraltar, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Yemen and Somalia, plus a stretch of the Indian Ocean. Around 89 million people live inside the path of totality — an enormous number compared with most eclipses, whose tracks fall over ocean or empty tundra.

The reach. Partial phases will be visible across nearly all of Europe, the whole of North Africa, the Middle East, and much of South and West Asia — including India.

What actually happens

A solar eclipse is a geometry problem. The Moon is about 400 times smaller than the Sun and about 400 times closer, which means the two appear almost exactly the same size in our sky. That coincidence is not guaranteed anywhere else in the solar system, and it is the reason total eclipses are possible at all.

When the Moon slides exactly between Earth and the Sun, its shadow falls on a small patch of the planet. That shadow has two parts. The penumbra is wide — thousands of kilometres across — and inside it you see a partial eclipse. The umbra is the dark core, and it is narrow: in 2027 it is only about 258 kilometres wide. Only people inside that strip see the Sun completely covered.

The Moon's shadow sweeps across the Earth's surface at roughly 1,000 to 2,000 kilometres per hour, which is why totality at any single location lasts only minutes even though the eclipse as a whole takes more than five hours to cross the planet.

Why this one lasts so long

Three orbital coincidences line up in 2027.

First, the Moon is close. It reaches perigee — its closest point to Earth — about two and a half hours after the eclipse, so its apparent disc is unusually large and covers the Sun generously.

Second, the Earth is near aphelion, its farthest point from the Sun, which happens in early July. That makes the Sun appear slightly smaller than average.

A bigger Moon and a smaller Sun means more overlap, and more overlap means a longer totality. This is the same mechanism that makes some eclipses short and others long.

Third, the path geometry puts the point of greatest eclipse over land. The eclipse belongs to Saros 136, a family of eclipses that repeat every 18 years and 11 days, and this particular member of the family happens to peak in exactly the right place.

The path, city by city

The shadow first touches Earth in the eastern Atlantic, then races east.

  • Spain — Cádiz gets 2 minutes 56 seconds, Marbella 3:21, Ceuta 4:49, Málaga just 1:57 (it sits on the edge of the path), Melilla 4:34
  • Gibraltar — 4:28
  • Morocco — Tangier 4:51
  • Algeria — Oran 5:08, M'sila 5:23
  • Tunisia — Kasserine 5:28, Sfax 5:41, Gabès 4:08
  • Libya — Benghazi 6:11
  • Egypt — Siwa Oasis 5:33, Asyut 6:07, Sohag 6:23, Nag Hammadi 6:24, Qena 6:12, Luxor 6:22
  • Saudi Arabia — Jeddah 5:53, Mecca 5:10, Khamis Mushait 6:00
  • Yemen — Sana'a 2:39
  • Somalia — Bosaso 4:08
  • British Indian Ocean Territory — Diego Garcia, near the end of the track

At Luxor the local timetable is roughly: partial phase begins at 11:40, totality from 13:02 to 13:08, maximum at 13:05, and the partial phase ends at 14:26 — nearly three hours of event, six minutes of the thing everyone is there for.

Notice how much the duration varies along the same path. Málaga gets under two minutes; Nag Hammadi, less than 500 kilometres away along the track, gets over six. Being "in the path" is not enough — you want to be near the centre line.

What you will actually see

Descriptions of totality always sound exaggerated until you have seen one. Here is the sequence.

First contact. A tiny bite appears in the Sun's edge. Over the next hour the bite grows, and the light turns strange — thin, silvery, slightly wrong. Shadows on the ground develop odd ripples as the atmosphere reacts.

The crescent thins. In the last few minutes before totality the Sun becomes a sliver. The light fades fast now, and the temperature starts dropping — often by five to ten degrees within minutes.

Baily's beads. Just before the Sun disappears, its light shines through valleys on the Moon's jagged edge, breaking into a string of bright points. This lasts seconds.

The diamond ring. One final bead remains, and the corona begins to appear around it — the most photographed second of any eclipse.

Totality. The Sun's disc is gone. What remains is the corona — the Sun's outer atmosphere, a million degrees hot but so thin it is normally invisible against the glare. It appears as pearly streamers, and its shape changes with the solar cycle.

The 360-degree sunset. The horizon in every direction glows orange, because you are standing inside the Moon's shadow and looking out at sunlight a few dozen kilometres away.

Stars and planets. Venus is usually obvious. In 2027 Mercury will be bright at magnitude −1.2, Jupiter sits near Regulus, Mars is further east in Virgo, and Saturn is well to the west. Over most of the path the Winter Hexagon will be visible.

Baily's beads again, in reverse. Totality ends with a second diamond ring, and then the light comes back far faster than it left.

Animals notice. Birds go quiet, insects change their behaviour, and in at least a few recorded cases nocturnal animals have briefly emerged.

What India will see

India is not in the path of totality in 2027. What you will get is a partial eclipse.

According to published timetables, the partial phase runs from about 3:34 pm to 5:53 pm IST on 2 August 2027. The western and north-western states — Rajasthan, Gujarat, Maharashtra and Goa — will see the most, with roughly 10 to 30 per cent of the Sun covered. The further east and north you go, the less you see.

Two practical warnings for Indian observers. First, the eclipse happens late in the afternoon, so in coastal cities the Sun will be low and may set before the eclipse finishes. Second, and much more important: because India only sees a partial eclipse, there is no moment when it is safe to look at the Sun without a filter. Not at maximum, not for a second.

ISRO, Vigyan Prasar and the Planetary Society of India have historically organised public viewing events and live streams for eclipses, and similar arrangements are expected for 2027.

There is also a cultural dimension. In India, eclipses carry religious significance, with temples often closing and observances tied to Rahu and Ketu. In recent years scientific outreach has run alongside tradition rather than against it, and many families now combine both — observing the rituals and watching the eclipse safely through filters.

How to watch it safely

This is the part that matters most, and it is not complicated.

Use ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses. That is the international standard for solar filters. Anything else — ordinary sunglasses, smoked glass, exposed film, CDs, polaroid scraps — is not safe, no matter how dark it looks.

Never look through a telescope, binoculars or a camera viewfinder at the Sun without a certified solar filter fitted to the front. Optical instruments concentrate light, and unfiltered sunlight through a lens will damage your retina in a fraction of a second.

If you are in the path of totality, you may remove the glasses only during the period of total coverage — and only then. The instant a sliver of the Sun reappears, put them back on.

Indirect viewing always works. A pinhole projector — a sheet of card with a small hole, projecting the Sun's image onto another sheet — is completely safe, needs no equipment, and shows the crescent shape clearly. A colander or the gaps between leaves does the same thing.

Cameras need filters too. Pointing a phone at the Sun without a filter can damage the sensor. For the partial phases, fit a solar filter over the lens.

What eclipses have given science

Total eclipses are not just spectacle. For 150 years they were the only way to study the Sun's corona, and they produced two discoveries that changed physics.

In 1868, during an eclipse visible from India, astronomers spectroscopically observed a bright yellow line in the corona that matched no known element. It turned out to be helium — found in the Sun before it was found on Earth.

In 1919, Arthur Eddington's expedition to Príncipe and Sobral measured starlight bending around the Sun during totality. The deflection matched Einstein's general relativity rather than Newtonian predictions, and the result made Einstein famous.

Today, coronagraphs and spacecraft can study the corona continuously, but eclipses still offer something instruments cannot: an unobstructed, natural view of the innermost corona from the ground, at a scale no artificial occulter can reproduce.

If you want to see totality

You do not need to be an astronomer, but you do need to plan.

Pick a location near the centre line, not just inside the path. Nag Hammadi and Luxor in Egypt, Benghazi in Libya, and Khamis Mushait in Saudi Arabia all sit near the line of greatest duration.

Book early. August 2027 eclipse tourism is already well underway, and accommodation in Luxor and along the Spanish coast is being booked years in advance.

Have a weather backup. August in Egypt is reliably clear but brutally hot — expect 40°C and above. Spain and the Mediterranean have better temperatures but more risk of cloud. Some travellers book two locations and decide a few days out based on forecasts.

Give yourself margin. Arrive at least two days early. Flight delays, road closures and crowds are the usual reasons people miss totality, not astronomy.

Remember it is short. Six minutes sounds like a long time. It is not. Decide in advance whether you are watching, photographing, or both — trying to do all three usually means doing none well.

After 2027

The next total solar eclipses:

  • 22 July 2028 — Australia, including Sydney
  • 25 November 2030 — southern Africa and Australia
  • 30 March 2033 — Alaska and the Russian Arctic
  • 20 March 2034 — central Africa and South Asia
  • 2 September 2035 — China, Korea and Japan

India's next total solar eclipse is in the 2030s, and the wait is long enough that anyone who can travel in 2027 should consider it.

Frequently asked

When is the 2027 total solar eclipse? Monday, 2 August 2027. The shadow reaches Earth in the mid-morning Atlantic and leaves over the Indian Ocean in the afternoon.

Where is the best place to see it? Near Luxor in Egypt, where totality lasts about 6 minutes 22 seconds. The point of greatest duration is roughly 60 km south-east of the city.

How long is totality? Up to 6 minutes 23 seconds at maximum. Most locations along the path get between 2 and 6 minutes.

Will it be visible in India? Only as a partial eclipse, between about 3:34 pm and 5:53 pm IST, with 10–30 per cent coverage in the west and north-west.

Is it safe to watch in India? Only with ISO 12312-2 certified filters. Because India sees a partial eclipse, there is never a moment when it is safe to look directly.

Why is it called the eclipse of the century? Because it offers the longest totality over accessible land in more than a century, with no longer eclipse until 2114.

How many people live in the path? Around 89 million.

What if it is cloudy? You will experience darkness, a temperature drop and the 360-degree sunset, but you will not see the corona. Weather is the only part of an eclipse nobody can control.
