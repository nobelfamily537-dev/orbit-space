Hera Arrives at Dimorphos: Inspecting the Asteroid We Hit on Purpose

Updated 30 October 202612 min read
Share WhatsApp Telegram X Facebook

On 4 November 2026, a small spacecraft the size of a car will settle into orbit around an asteroid 197 million kilometres from Earth. It has taken two years to get there. When it opens its eyes properly, it will be looking at the first object in the Solar System whose path around its parent body was deliberately changed by human beings.

The spacecraft is Hera, built by the European Space Agency. The asteroid is Dimorphos. And four years ago, we hit it with a rocket on purpose.

What we did in 2022

On 26 September 2022, NASA's DART — the Double Asteroid Redirection Test — slammed into Dimorphos at 6.1 kilometres per second, roughly 22,000 kilometres per hour. It was not a flyby or a sample grab. The spacecraft was the weapon: a 570-kilogram impactor aimed at a 151-metre rock to see whether hitting something hard enough could nudge it off a collision course.

It worked better than almost anyone expected.

Dimorphos orbits a larger asteroid called Didymos. Before the impact, that orbit took 11 hours and 55 minutes. Afterwards it took about 11 hours and 22 minutes — a shift of roughly 33 minutes. The collision threw a plume of debris across 10,000 kilometres of space, and it changed not only how Dimorphos circles Didymos but the pair's orbit around the Sun.

That single result turned a theoretical planetary defence technique into a demonstrated one. If a dangerous asteroid were ever found heading for Earth, we now know that hitting it with a spacecraft can measurably change where it goes.

Why we needed a second mission

DART died on impact, which means it could never answer the questions that matter most.

Did the collision leave a clean crater, or did it reshape the entire asteroid? What is Dimorphos actually made of, and how much does it weigh? Why was the deflection so much larger than pre-impact models predicted? And is the asteroid now tumbling, or has material been moving between it and Didymos?

Those are not academic questions. Without the mass, you cannot calculate how efficiently momentum was transferred. And without that, you cannot say how big a spacecraft you would need to move a real threat.

"We know that there was a lot of material kicked up," said Angela Stickle of Johns Hopkins University's Applied Physics Laboratory, who worked on both missions. "We know that we probably left a pretty significant mark on Dimorphos." But how big a mark, and what shape, nobody knows.

Patrick Michel of CNRS, Hera's principal investigator, put the mission's job plainly: Hera wants to be the detective, like Inspector Columbo, going back to the crash scene.

First light: two asteroids instead of one

Hera launched on 7 October 2024. For most of the journey the Didymos system was a single point of light even in the spacecraft's camera.

That changed on the evening of 18 October 2026. From 1,280 kilometres away, Hera's monochromatic Asteroid Framing Camera resolved Didymos and Dimorphos as two separate objects for the first time. Dimorphos was barely a pixel, but it was unmistakably its own thing, sitting apart from its parent.

"With this image the smaller Dimorphos asteroid becomes visible for the first time," said Michael Kueppers. "Before this acquisition the spacecraft was still resolving the Didymos system as a single dot."

Mission manager Ian Carnelli was blunter about what it meant: "For the very first time we are seeing through Hera's own eyes the very double asteroid system that the mission was designed and built for."

How to park next to an asteroid

Arriving is harder than it sounds. Hera had been travelling at more than 12 kilometres per second relative to Earth. To stay near Dimorphos, it had to shed almost all of that relative velocity — without overshooting, and without relying on real-time commands, since the signal delay at 197 million kilometres makes joystick flying impossible.

The braking happened in stages.

15 October 2026 — the first Braking Manoeuvre, BRM-1. Hera rotated so its ring of Orbit Control Thrusters faced the asteroids, then fired three hydrazine thrusters continuously for 93 minutes. Reaction control thrusters worked the whole time to hold attitude.

22 October 2026 — BRM-2, a shorter burn of about 31 minutes, to finish most of the remaining velocity change and correct for any shortfall in the first.

Then three smaller transition manoeuvres through the end of October brought the relative velocity down to about 20 metres per second — walking pace, in space terms.

Only after all of that could Hera begin the approach. And this is where the mission gets genuinely new: Hera carries an autonomous "self-driving" navigation system that combines data from multiple sensors to keep the asteroid in frame, because the debris environment around Dimorphos is not fully mapped and nobody on Earth can steer in real time.

The investigation plan

The science starts in November with what ESA calls the Early Characterisation Phase, at a cautious distance of about 30 kilometres. By December Hera closes to roughly 10 kilometres, then 4, then as little as 1 kilometre.

ESA will publish the first detailed images of Dimorphos on 25 November 2026 — the first close look at the crash site since DART.

The instruments will map both asteroids, measure their mass and gravity field, and hunt for the crater. They will also look for pieces of DART itself, though nobody expects to find much: at 6.1 km/s, most of the spacecraft would have shattered or vaporised. The one faint possibility is that a fragment of its long solar panels survived — and those panels may have stretched the crater into an oval rather than a circle.

The question nobody can answer yet: crater, or no crater?

This is the heart of the mission, and there are two competing pictures.

The conventional expectation is a crater — a bowl punched into the asteroid by the impact.

But computer simulations run by Martin Jutzi and John Wimarsson at the University of Bern, with Sabina Raducan at the International Space Science Institute, suggest something stranger. If Dimorphos is not solid rock but a loosely bound pile of rubble held together by almost nothing, then a 6.1 km/s impact would not dig a neat hole. It would deform the entire asteroid.

If the Bern models are right, Hera will find Dimorphos reshaped rather than dented — and that changes the whole calculus of asteroid deflection, because a rubble pile absorbs and redistributes energy very differently from a solid body.

There is a second puzzle too. Cristina Thomas of Northern Arizona University, who worked on both missions, noted that Dimorphos' orbital period was still changing six months after impact, for reasons nobody could explain. "This thing that we thought was going to be very cut and dry turned out to be really interesting and complicated," she said.

Two CubeSats and a landing

Hera is not travelling alone. It carries two shoebox-sized CubeSats, to be deployed in early 2027.

Milani will study the composition of the material on and around Dimorphos — what the surface is made of, and what the impact scattered.

Juventas carries radar, and its job is the most ambitious of all: to peer inside an asteroid. No spacecraft has ever directly studied the interior of a small body before. Juventas is expected to end its mission by landing on Dimorphos; Milani may attempt the same.

Operating three independently flying spacecraft around a body with almost no gravity is genuinely difficult. Solar radiation pressure — the push of sunlight — is enough to noticeably alter a CubeSat's trajectory there. Hera will be the first mission to attempt keeping three separately operated spacecraft coordinated in that environment, and the collision risk is real.

How big are these things, really?

Scale helps. Dimorphos, at 151 metres across, is roughly the size of the Great Pyramid of Giza. Didymos, at 780 metres, is a little smaller than the Burj Khalifa is tall. The two orbit each other at an average distance of 1.15 kilometres, with Dimorphos crawling around its parent at about 18 centimetres per second — slower than a snail.

A 151-metre asteroid would not end civilisation. But it would destroy a city, and we currently have no tested way to stop one.

Why any of this matters

Asteroid impacts are not science fiction, they are statistics.

Objects roughly 10 to 20 metres across hit Earth every few decades — the Chelyabinsk airburst in 2013 was in this class, and it damaged thousands of buildings without anyone seeing it coming.

Objects of 100 to 200 metres — Dimorphos-sized — arrive on a timescale of roughly every 10,000 to 20,000 years, according to Jutzi. Those are the ones that cause regional or even global devastation. Not extinction-level, but bad enough that "we should probably have a plan" is a reasonable position.

DART proved the plan can work. Hera is how we find out how well, and for what kind of asteroid.

What happens next

  • 4 November 2026 — arrival at the Didymos system
  • 25 November 2026 — first detailed images of Dimorphos released
  • December 2026 — approach to about 10 km, then closer
  • January 2027 — Milani and Juventas deployed
  • Early 2027 — close survey, interior radar, landing attempts
  • Through at least September 2027 — main spacecraft continues operations

Frequently asked

What is Hera? A European Space Agency planetary defence mission that launched on 7 October 2024 to study the aftermath of NASA's DART impact on the asteroid Dimorphos.

When does Hera arrive? 4 November 2026, after braking manoeuvres in October 2026.

What did DART do? On 26 September 2022 it struck Dimorphos at 6.1 km/s, shortening the asteroid's orbit around Didymos by about 33 minutes — the first time humanity deliberately changed the motion of a Solar System body.

Why not just use DART's data? DART was destroyed on impact. It could not measure the mass, structure or crater it left behind, and those are the numbers needed to turn a demonstration into an operational technique.

How big is Dimorphos? About 151 metres across — roughly the Great Pyramid of Giza.

Will Hera land on the asteroid? The main spacecraft will not. Its CubeSat Juventas is expected to attempt a landing, and Milani may also try.

Is this about an asteroid that threatens Earth? No. Didymos and Dimorphos pose no danger. They are a test range — a pair of ordinary near-Earth asteroids chosen because they are reachable and because Dimorphos is small enough to move.
