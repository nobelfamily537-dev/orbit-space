Europa Clipper: Hunting for Life Under Europa's Ice

Updated 3 November 20267 min read
Share WhatsApp Telegram X Facebook

Europa is a small moon of Jupiter, a little smaller than our own Moon, with almost no atmosphere and a surface temperature that never climbs above about −160°C. It is also the single most promising place in the solar system to look for life beyond Earth. NASA built Europa Clipper to find out whether that promise is real.

The spacecraft launched on 14 October 2024 on a SpaceX Falcon Heavy and is now in the middle of a six-year cruise. It reaches Jupiter in April 2030, and then it will spend years sweeping past Europa again and again, mapping the ice and peering underneath it.

Why Europa of all places?

Because underneath that cracked, bright shell of ice there is water. Not a little ice, not a frozen relic, but a global ocean of liquid water wrapped around the whole moon. Estimates put its depth somewhere between 60 and 150 kilometres, sitting beneath an ice crust roughly 15 to 25 kilometres thick. Earth's oceans average less than 4 kilometres deep, so Europa may hold close to twice the water of every ocean on Earth combined.

That ocean has probably been liquid for most of the solar system's history. Four and a half billion years of liquid water is a very long time for chemistry to do interesting things.

What keeps it from freezing

Sunlight cannot do it. Jupiter is more than five times farther from the Sun than Earth, and the light arriving there is roughly twenty-five times weaker. Instead, the heat comes from Jupiter's gravity.

Europa's orbit is slightly elliptical, and the strength of Jupiter's pull changes as the moon moves closer and farther away. The result is that Europa is squeezed and stretched on every orbit, flexing its interior like a ball of dough. That constant kneading produces friction, and friction produces heat. The same mechanism is what drives the volcanoes of Io, the moon next door.

The ingredients for life

Liquid water is only one part of the recipe. Life as we know it also needs chemistry: the right elements, an energy source, and something to build with. Europa appears to have all three, at least in principle.

Its rocky interior is thought to be in contact with the ocean, which would supply minerals. Radiation at the surface splits water molecules apart and creates oxidising chemicals, and the surface is young enough that material may be recycled down into the ocean through cracks and chaos terrain. If that happens, the ocean gets a chemical battery delivered from above.

There is also the question of plumes. Hubble observations suggested water vapour erupting from Europa's south polar region, and if those plumes are real, a spacecraft flying through one could sample the ocean directly without ever touching the ice.

The spacecraft

Europa Clipper is the largest spacecraft NASA has ever sent to another planet. With its solar arrays unfolded it spans more than 30 metres, longer than a basketball court, and it weighs around 6,000 kilograms with propellant. Those enormous arrays exist because sunlight at Jupiter is so weak: they need to be huge to gather enough power for the instruments.

Nine instruments, one question

The payload is built around a single question: is the ocean habitable? Each instrument answers a different part of it.

A radar sounder called REASON will penetrate the ice and measure how thick it is, and where the ocean begins. A thermal imager, E-THEMIS, will hunt for warm spots where water might be close to the surface. Mass spectrometers will taste the chemistry of the surface and of any plume material. Cameras will map the moon at far higher resolution than anything before, including the strange chaos terrain where the ice has clearly broken and refrozen. A magnetometer will measure the induced magnetic field, which is one of the cleanest ways to confirm a salty ocean, because a conductor moving through Jupiter's magnetic field generates its own signature.

The radiation problem

Europa sits inside Jupiter's radiation belts, and the dose at the surface would kill a human in about a day. Electronics do not survive there for long either. This is the central engineering problem of the mission.

The solution is a vault: a thick-walled box of titanium and aluminium that shields the most sensitive electronics. On the outside of that vault sits a commemorative plate carrying a poem by Ada Limón and the word for water in more than a hundred languages, plus a silicon chip with hundreds of thousands of names submitted by the public.

49 flybys, not one orbit

Rather than orbit Europa, which would park the spacecraft in the worst of the radiation, Clipper will orbit Jupiter and make about 49 close passes of the moon. Each flyby lasts a few hours. Between passes the spacecraft retreats to a safer distance to transmit data and recharge.

It is an unusual way to run a mission, but it solves two problems at once: it limits radiation exposure and it lets the spacecraft cover the whole moon instead of one narrow band.

The road to Jupiter

Clipper cannot fly straight to Jupiter; it is too heavy and the rocket cannot throw it that far. Instead it borrows energy from planets. It flew past Mars in March 2025, picking up speed, and will swing past Earth in December 2026 for another boost. It arrives at Jupiter in April 2030. The prime mission runs for roughly four years after that.

What happens if it finds something

Clipper will not find life. It is not built to. What it can do is tell us whether the conditions are right, which is the question that has to be answered first.

If the answer is yes, the next step would be a lander that puts instruments directly onto the ice and, eventually, something that goes through it. Europe's JUICE spacecraft is also on its way to Jupiter, arriving in 2031, and between the two missions we will learn more about the Jovian system in the next decade than in the previous four hundred years.

For now, a spacecraft the size of a basketball court is falling towards Jupiter at tens of thousands of kilometres per hour, carrying nine instruments and one very old question: are we alone?
