Apophis 2029: The Asteroid That Will Pass Closer Than Our Satellites

Updated 16 November 20269 min read
Share WhatsApp Telegram X Facebook

On 13 April 2029 an asteroid about 340 metres across will pass closer to Earth than the satellites that carry our television signals. It will not hit us. It will be visible to the naked eye for roughly seven hours, and something like nine out of every ten people on the planet will be standing in a place where they could, in principle, look up and see it.

Nothing like this has happened in recorded history. We have watched asteroids pass before, through telescopes. We have never been able to predict, decades in advance, that one would come close enough to be seen without instruments.

What Apophis is

99942 Apophis is named after the Egyptian god of chaos and destruction, which tells you how the people who discovered it were feeling. It was found in June 2004, and by December of that year the orbit calculations had it at roughly a one-in-thirty-seven chance of hitting Earth on 13 April 2029.

That made it the most hazardous asteroid known at the time. It is also why the object is famous: for a few years, Apophis was the asteroid, the one in the documentaries, the one that made people ask what would happen.

Then the observations caught up. Radar measurements in 2021, and optical work after that, refined the orbit enough to rule out a 2029 impact entirely, and to rule out any impact for at least the next hundred years.

The numbers

The closest approach happens at about 21:45 UTC, when Apophis passes roughly 31,600 kilometres above the North Atlantic.

That is inside the ring where geostationary satellites sit, which is about 35,800 kilometres up. An asteroid roughly the size of a football stadium will come closer to the surface of Earth than the machines we use for weather forecasting and broadcast television.

It is, in the words of MIT planetary scientist Richard Binzel, a once-per-millennium natural experiment.

What you will actually see

This is the part people get wrong. Apophis will not streak across the sky like a meteor. It is not entering the atmosphere, so there is no fireball and no tail.

It will look like a point of light, about as bright as a fairly ordinary star, moving steadily against the background. It crosses the sky in hours rather than minutes, and at closest approach it moves by roughly the apparent width of the full moon every minute. Slow enough to notice, fast enough that you can see it move if you watch for a few seconds.

The visibility window opens over Australia at about 15:00 UTC and closes over the North Atlantic around 22:00 UTC. It reaches its greatest apparent brightness at roughly 20:35 UTC while passing over Cameroon, and at that moment an estimated 3.9 billion people across Africa, Asia, eastern South America and parts of Europe are in the viewing zone.

Can you see it from India?

India sits inside the visibility band, but the timing is awkward. Greatest brightness is 20:35 UTC, which is about 2:05 in the morning on 14 April in India. Apophis will be a naked-eye object somewhere above the horizon, but how high depends on where you are, and a low altitude plus city light pollution can easily hide a star-bright point.

The honest answer is: check a planetarium app a few days before, once the final tracking predictions are published. Any app that carries fresh asteroid ephemerides will tell you exactly where to look from your city, at what time, and how high it sits. Do not rely on a number written months in advance.

What it will do to the asteroid

Earth's gravity will bend Apophis onto a new orbit around the Sun. That part is certain and harmless.

What is not certain is what the close pass does to the asteroid itself. The same gravitational forces that redirect it will squeeze and stretch it slightly. That could be enough to trigger landslides on its surface, or to crack open weathered rock and expose fresh material underneath. It could also do almost nothing.

"We simply don't know what's going to happen," Binzel said. "Apophis may go by and not care too much, or maybe we'll see something significant."

OSIRIS-APEX will be there

The spacecraft formerly known as OSIRIS-REx, which brought samples of the asteroid Bennu back to Earth in 2023, is still flying. It has been renamed OSIRIS-APEX and retargeted for Apophis.

It will arrive shortly after the flyby and go into orbit, then perform a manoeuvre that fires its thrusters close to the surface to disturb the regolith and see what lies underneath. It is the first mission designed to watch what a close planetary encounter does to an asteroid.

How big is 340 metres, really

Big enough to matter, small enough to be misleading.

In 1908 an object perhaps 50 to 60 metres across exploded over Tunguska in Siberia and flattened around 2,000 square kilometres of forest. In 2013 a roughly 20-metre object came in over Chelyabinsk and the shockwave shattered windows across a region, sending about 1,500 people to hospital, mostly from broken glass.

Apophis is several times larger than the Tunguska object. The energy scales steeply with size, so the difference between 50 metres and 340 metres is not a factor of seven, it is far more.

And we now know we are not helpless. In 2022 NASA's DART mission deliberately crashed a spacecraft into the asteroid Dimorphos and measurably changed its orbit. Deflection works, at least for objects of that size, given enough warning. Apophis is not a target — it does not need deflecting — but it is the reason the question is no longer purely theoretical.

Is it dangerous?

No. The 2029 pass is safe, the orbit is well known, and the impact risk for the rest of this century has been ruled out.

What remains is a rare piece of luck: an object big enough to be interesting, close enough to see, predicted far enough in advance that we can plan for it. Astronomers are already mapping exactly where in the sky it will appear, from which cities, at what minute.

On the evening of 13 April 2029, most of the world will be able to walk outside and watch a 340-metre rock go past. That has never happened before, and it will not happen again in our lifetimes.
