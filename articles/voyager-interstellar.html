Voyager: The Farthest Objects Humans Have Ever Built

Updated 12 November 20269 min read
Share WhatsApp Telegram X Facebook

In August and September 1977 NASA launched two spacecraft on a mission designed to last five years. Nearly half a century later both are still flying, still talking to Earth, and still the farthest objects humans have ever built.

Voyager 1 is roughly 167 times farther from the Sun than Earth is. Its radio signal, travelling at the speed of light, takes more than twenty-three hours to reach us. When the antennas on Earth receive that signal, the spacecraft has already moved on.

These two machines were built to look at Jupiter and Saturn. What they actually did was redraw the solar system.

A mission that almost did not happen

The idea began in the late 1960s, when engineers noticed that the outer planets would line up in a rare configuration in the late 1970s and 1980s. A spacecraft launched at the right moment could use each planet's gravity as a slingshot to reach the next one, turning a multi-decade tour into a single flight.

That alignment happens roughly once every 176 years. NASA built two spacecraft instead of one, partly as insurance and partly because the opportunity was too good to waste on a single shot.

Voyager 2 launched first, on 20 August 1977. Voyager 1 launched on 5 September 1977 and, being on a faster trajectory, overtook its twin before either reached Jupiter.

What they found

At Jupiter in 1979 the spacecraft discovered that the moon Io is volcanically active, the first time active volcanoes had been seen anywhere other than Earth. They found faint rings around Jupiter, and they watched the Great Red Spot in detail for the first time.

At Saturn, Voyager 1 flew past in November 1980 and found a rings system far more intricate than anyone expected, including braided and kinked ringlets and strange spoke features. It also took a close look at Titan, Saturn's largest moon, which is why it was sent on a path that ended its planetary tour.

Voyager 2 went on to Uranus in January 1986 and Neptune in August 1989. It remains the only spacecraft ever to visit either world. It found that Uranus's magnetic field is badly tilted, discovered ten new moons at Uranus, and at Neptune it photographed the Great Dark Spot and found geysers erupting from the surface of Triton.

Between them, the two spacecraft discovered or co-discovered more than twenty moons.

The Pale Blue Dot

In February 1990, at the request of Carl Sagan, Voyager 1 turned its cameras back towards the Sun and took a series of images that became a mosaic of the solar system. In one of those frames, Earth appears as a single pixel, a pale blue dot caught in a band of scattered sunlight.

Nothing about that photograph is scientifically unusual. Everything about it is a reminder of scale. The cameras were switched off shortly afterwards to save power, and they have never been turned on again.

Crossing the boundary

For most of their lives the Voyagers have been inside the heliosphere, the bubble of charged particles and magnetic field blown outward by the Sun. Beyond it lies interstellar space, filled with the plasma and magnetic field of the galaxy itself.

Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause on 25 August 2012. Voyager 2 followed on 5 November 2018, and because Voyager 2 carries a working plasma spectrometer it sent back the first direct measurements of the boundary.

They are now the first and only spacecraft operating in interstellar space. Nothing is chasing them. No mission is planned to overtake them.

How they are still alive

Both spacecraft run on radioisotope thermoelectric generators, which convert the heat of decaying plutonium into electricity. There are no solar panels, because sunlight that far out is useless.

Every year the generators produce about four watts less. The team has spent the last decade switching off instruments and heaters in a careful order, keeping the essential systems alive as long as possible. In 2023 and 2024 engineers found ways to keep Voyager 1 running by managing its power budget more aggressively than anyone thought possible.

The expectation is that both will fall silent somewhere in the 2030s. When that happens they will keep flying, carrying the Golden Record, for longer than the human species has existed in its current form.

The Golden Record

Both spacecraft carry an identical gold-plated copper disk, twelve inches across, designed to be playable by anyone who finds it. The cover is engraved with instructions for playing it, a map showing the location of Earth using pulsars as reference points, and a diagram of the hydrogen atom.

The contents were chosen by a committee chaired by Carl Sagan. There are 115 images encoded as analogue video, greetings in 55 languages, a recording of Earth sounds from wind and surf to a heartbeat, and ninety minutes of music ranging from Bach and Beethoven to Chuck Berry and Bulgarian folk singing.

It is, by any measure, an act of optimism. The odds of anyone ever finding it are vanishingly small, and it was made anyway.

Where they are now

Voyager 1 is heading roughly towards the constellation Ophiuchus. Voyager 2 is heading towards Pavo. In about 40,000 years Voyager 1 will pass within 1.6 light years of the star Gliese 445, and in about 296,000 years Voyager 2 will pass near Sirius.

They will not be recovered. They will not report back. They are simply the farthest things we have ever thrown, and they are still, faintly, saying hello.
