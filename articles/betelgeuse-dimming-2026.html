Betelgeuse Is Dimming Again, and Astronomers Are Watching Closely

Updated 27 November 202611 min read
Share WhatsApp Telegram X

In October 2026, a team of astronomers filed a short, urgent note to the Astronomer's Telegram. Betelgeuse, the orange star on Orion's shoulder, had been fading since August. Between then and early October its brightness had dropped from magnitude 0.5 to about 0.95. More tellingly, the star's upper atmosphere had started to fall inward, layer by layer, at a rate that matched the weeks before its famous collapse in 2020.

The note's title was a question: is another dimming event imminent?

It is a good question. And the answer tells us a lot about what happens to a star in the last hundred thousand years of its life.

What is happening right now

Betelgeuse is a red supergiant: a star roughly seventeen times the mass of the Sun that has finished burning hydrogen in its core and swollen to enormous size. Its surface is not solid. It is a boiling, churning layer of gas, and it is losing mass constantly.

Since August 2026, high-resolution spectroscopy from the STELLA observatory has shown the star's inner atmospheric layers falling inward steeply, with the outermost layer beginning to follow. The velocity difference between the innermost and outermost layers reached about −5.5 kilometres per second by 20 September. Since 2008, the only other occasion with a larger gradient was the Great Dimming itself.

The time delay between the layers is also similar: about two months. If the pattern repeats, the darkest point would arrive around November 2026, which is now.

That does not mean the star is about to explode. It means the star is being a red supergiant, which is a noisy business.

What the Great Dimming actually was

Between December 2019 and February 2020, Betelgeuse dropped to magnitude 1.6, its faintest in more than a century. The internet briefly lost its composure. It was not a supernova.

Two lines of evidence explained it. In June 2021, a team led by Miguel Montargès published Nature results from the Very Large Telescope showing that a patch of the star's surface had cooled, and that gas thrown off earlier had condensed into dust on the line of sight, partly veiling the star. Separately, Andrea Dupree's team using Hubble found evidence of a surface mass ejection, a blob of material hundreds of billions of times more massive than a typical solar coronal mass ejection.

So the dimming was local weather, not a death notice. The star was still shining at roughly the same luminosity in the infrared. Only the visible light was being blocked.

The star itself

The numbers are hard to hold in your head, so here they are plainly.

Betelgeuse sits about 550 light-years away, with estimates ranging roughly from 500 to 640. It is about 764 times the radius of the Sun. Put it where the Sun is and its surface would reach beyond the orbit of Mars, most of the way toward Jupiter. Its mass is roughly 16.5 to 19 solar masses, and it is currently fusing helium in its core.

Because it is so large and puffy, it has no sharp edge. That is one reason its distance is difficult to pin down, and why different measurements disagree by tens of light-years.

The companion nobody could see

For a century, Betelgeuse has had a second brightness cycle of about six years that did not fit its pulsations. A companion star tugging on it was the leading explanation, and in the last two years the evidence finally arrived.

In 2024, two teams independently modelled the six-year cycle as an orbiting companion. Jared Goldberg and colleagues estimated a mass around 1.2 solar masses on a period of about 2,170 days. In May 2025, Chandra saw no X-rays where the companion should be, and Hubble saw nothing in ultraviolet, which ruled out a feeding white dwarf or neutron star and argued against anything heavier than about 1.5 solar masses.

In July 2025, Steve Howell and colleagues used the speckle imager at Gemini North to spot a faint source 52 milliarcseconds from Betelgeuse, about six magnitudes fainter. It was a 1.5-sigma detection, so the team called it probable rather than certain.

The companion now has a name: Siwarha, usually translated as her bracelet.

In January 2026, Dupree's team reported signs of the companion's wake in eight years of spectra, as the small star ploughs through Betelgeuse's extended atmosphere. And in July 2026, Montargès and colleagues re-analysed VLT/SPHERE images taken in December 2024 and found the companion at 52.3 milliarcseconds with a significance of about six sigma. If the two stars formed together, its brightness implies roughly 2.6 to 3.1 solar masses, considerably more than the earlier predictions.

It is still not fully confirmed. A second observation, with the companion on the other side of Betelgeuse, is needed to prove it is bound in orbit. An August 2026 preprint argues that some of the light may be Betelgeuse's own, reflected off dust around the companion, which would allow a lighter star consistent with the Hubble limit. The next good chance to see the pair at their widest separation comes around November 2027.

When will it explode

Almost certainly not tonight.

A 2020 study led by Meridith Joyce matched Betelgeuse's pulsations to stellar evolution models and concluded that a star of its mass, still burning helium, has roughly 100,000 years of life left. That is the mainstream view.

In 2023, Hideyuki Saio and colleagues proposed that Betelgeuse had already moved on to burning carbon, which would bring the end much closer, with core collapse within a few centuries at most. A reply by László Molnár, Joyce and Shing-Chi Leung pointed out that those models require a radius that conflicts with the star's measured angular size. The question is not formally closed, but most astronomers favour the longer timeline.

When it does go, it will be a core-collapse supernova, the kind described by Baade and Zwicky in 1934. What remains will be a neutron star, or possibly a black hole.

What it would look like from here

Betelgeuse is about 550 light-years away. That is close enough to be spectacular and far enough to be harmless. The dangerous radius for a supernova is usually quoted as roughly 50 to 100 light-years, and we are well outside it.

From Earth, the explosion would be unmissable. It would reach roughly the brightness of a half-moon, visible in daylight for weeks and bright enough to cast shadows at night. It would stay visible to the naked eye for months, and would slowly fade over a couple of years into a supernova remnant.

There is no recorded supernova in the Milky Way since 1604, the one Kepler saw. A Betelgeuse supernova would be the first in the telescopic age, and the most-observed astronomical event in human history.

Why this dimming matters

Nobody expects a supernova in November 2026. What astronomers are actually watching is something rarer: a star's atmosphere in the act of reorganising itself, at a resolution and cadence we have never had before.

Red supergiants are the factories where much of the carbon and oxygen in the universe was made and thrown out. Their mass loss shapes the space around them and seeds the next generation of stars. But the mechanism is poorly understood, because the stars are enormous, close, and difficult to model.

Betelgeuse is the closest red supergiant we have. Every dimming event is a natural experiment, and this is the second one in six years. Whatever the next few weeks bring, the data will be worth having.

If it fades, look up. If it does not, look up anyway. It is a star that will end, and we happen to be alive at a moment when we can watch it think about it.
