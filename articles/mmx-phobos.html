MMX: Japan's Mission to Bring Back a Piece of Mars' Moon

Updated 26 October 202611 min read
Share WhatsApp Telegram X Facebook

On 20 October 2026, at 04:41 Japan Standard Time, an H3 rocket lifted off from Tanegashima Space Center carrying a spacecraft with one of the most ambitious itineraries ever attempted. Its name is MMX — Martian Moons eXploration — and if it works, it will do something no mission has ever done: bring a piece of the Martian system back to Earth.

Not Mars itself. Mars's moon, Phobos.

The spacecraft is led by JAXA, Japan's space agency, in collaboration with NASA, France's CNES, Germany's DLR and the European Space Agency. It carries thirteen instruments, a small rover, two different sampling devices and a return capsule. It will spend five years getting there, working there and coming home.

Why Phobos?

Mars has two moons, Phobos and Deimos, and both are strange. They are tiny — Phobos is about 22 kilometres across — dark, and shaped like lumpy potatoes. They orbit close to Mars and move fast.

The puzzle is that nobody knows where they came from. There are two competing explanations, and they have been argued about for decades.

The first is that they are captured asteroids — small bodies from the asteroid belt that strayed too close to Mars and were pulled into orbit. This is the intuitive answer, and it fits their dark, carbon-rich appearance.

The second is that they formed from debris after a giant impact — a large object smashed into Mars early in its history, threw a disk of material into orbit, and that material clumped together into the two moons. This would make them genuinely Martian in origin rather than captured visitors.

The two theories predict different things about what Phobos is made of. A captured asteroid should look like asteroid material, with a composition matching known meteorites. An impact-formed moon should contain material mixed from Mars itself.

That is a question you cannot answer from orbit. You need to bring a piece of it home and measure it in a laboratory, with instruments far more sensitive than anything that can be flown on a spacecraft. That is the entire reason MMX exists.

The journey

MMX's route is not a straight line, because Mars sits deep in the Sun's gravity well and getting there and back takes a large rocket and a large spacecraft. The mission's total mass is about 4,480 kilograms and it is built in three modules: a propulsion module, an exploration module with landing legs, and a return module holding the sample capsule.

  • October 2026 — launch from Tanegashima on an H3
  • August 2027 — arrival at Mars, insertion into orbit
  • September 2027 — enters a quasi-satellite orbit around Phobos
  • 2027 to 2029 — detailed observation of Phobos, and scouting for landing and sampling sites
  • Early 2029 — the IDEFIX rover is released onto the surface
  • 2029 to 2030 — two landings by the main spacecraft to collect samples
  • November 2030 — departure from the Martian system, with flybys of Deimos and Mars on the way out
  • 2031 — the return capsule lands in the Woomera Prohibited Area, Australia

The Mars orbit insertion is described by the mission team as the greatest challenge of the first half. The departure burn — escaping the Martian sphere in one go — is the most tense moment of the second.

IDEFIX: the rover that barely weighs anything

Before the main spacecraft lands, a small rover goes first. IDEFIX is a 25-kilogram rover built by DLR and CNES, and it has two jobs: characterise the surface so the main spacecraft can land safely, and analyse the composition and texture of the regolith.

It will operate for about 100 days. Its top speed is roughly one millimetre per second — not a typo. It carries a radiometer, cameras and a Raman spectrometer, and it produces at least 60 watt-hours of power per Phobos day, which lasts about seven hours.

The detail that makes the whole thing tangible: gravity on Phobos is roughly 1,800 times weaker than on Earth. That 25-kilogram rover would weigh about 15 grams on the surface. Driving on a world where your vehicle weighs as much as a couple of sheets of paper is a genuinely new engineering problem, and IDEFIX is the first attempt to solve it.

Collecting the sample

The main spacecraft will land on Phobos twice, at two different sites, and collect material both times. It has two sampling systems:

  • C-SMP, a coring sampler that drills to a depth of more than two centimetres
  • P-SMP, a pneumatic sampler that collects loose surface material

The target is at least 10 grams of material. For context, Hayabusa2 aimed to collect 0.1 grams from asteroid Ryugu and came back with 5.4 grams. MMX is asking for considerably more, from a body with gravity strong enough to make landing harder than on an asteroid but weak enough that everything about the descent has to be done slowly.

Ten grams sounds tiny. It is not. Sample-return scientists can extract extraordinary amounts of information from microscopic quantities — grain by grain, isotope by isotope — using laboratory instruments that would never survive a launch.

The instruments

MMX carries thirteen instruments, eleven of them for science. Seven are on the main spacecraft and four are on the rover, with several contributed by international partners.

The remote-sensing and in-situ suite includes TENGOO (a telescopic camera for high-resolution surface imaging), OROCHI (a wide-angle multispectral camera), MIRS (a near-infrared spectrometer from CNES for rock, water and organic material), MEGANE (a NASA gamma-ray and neutron spectrometer for elemental composition), LIDAR (a laser altimeter) and MSA (an ion mass spectrometer hunting for possible ice deposits). Two more instruments — a dust monitor and a radiation monitor — support engineering and future crewed exploration.

The combination matters. Remote sensing tells you what the surface looks like and roughly what it is made of. The returned sample tells you precisely, and lets scientists check their remote readings against ground truth for decades afterwards.

What the samples could tell us

There are three big questions MMX is designed to address.

Where did the Martian moons come from? This is the headline. Measuring the isotopic composition of Phobos material and comparing it with Mars and with known asteroids should settle the capture-versus-impact argument.

What can the moons tell us about habitability? If Phobos contains water-bearing minerals or organic material, it tells us something about how volatiles moved around the early inner solar system — the same question that missions like Hayabusa2 and OSIRIS-REx have been chasing at asteroids.

How has Mars and its environment changed? Phobos has been sitting next to Mars for billions of years. Its surface may preserve a record of material ejected from Mars over that time, including material from periods when Mars was warmer and wetter.

There is also a practical angle the mission team is explicit about: a round trip to the Martian system is exactly the kind of capability that future Mars sample return — and eventually human missions — will need. MMX is proving out the transport, the sampling and the return.

Why this is a genuinely hard mission

It is worth pausing on how much has to go right.

Landing on a body with almost no gravity means you cannot rely on friction or weight to hold you down. The spacecraft has to touch down, stay put long enough to drill, and lift off again — twice. The sampling mechanism has to work in vacuum, in dust, on a surface nobody has touched. The return capsule has to survive re-entry at interplanetary speed and land in a specific patch of the Australian outback.

Japan has done parts of this before. Hayabusa and Hayabusa2 both returned samples from asteroids, and that heritage is the reason this mission is credible rather than speculative. But Mars is much further, the gravity well is much deeper, and the moon is a different kind of target entirely.

Frequently asked

What is MMX? The Martian Moons eXploration mission, led by JAXA, to study Phobos and Deimos and return samples from Phobos to Earth.

When did MMX launch? 20 October 2026 at 04:41 JST from Tanegashima Space Center on an H3 rocket — 19 October in Europe and the Americas.

When will the samples come back? The return capsule is expected to land in Australia in 2031.

How much sample will it collect? At least 10 grams, from a depth of more than two centimetres, across two landing sites.

Has anything ever returned a sample from a Martian moon? No. MMX would be the first mission to return material from the Martian system.

Is it only Japan? No. JAXA leads, with NASA, CNES, DLR and ESA as partners. The IDEFIX rover is built by DLR and CNES.
